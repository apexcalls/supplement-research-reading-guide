Reading Supplement Research: A Buyer's Guide to Studies

Supplement brands cite research the way lawyers cite precedent: selectively, and in their own favour. A label that says "backed by science" may rest on a large randomised trial, a tiny pilot study, or a laboratory experiment on cells. The citation looks the same in each case. Learning to tell them apart is one of the most useful skills a supplement buyer can develop.

Start with study design. The strongest evidence comes from randomised controlled trials: participants are randomly assigned to receive the ingredient or a placebo, and neither they nor the researchers know who received what. This design controls for placebo effects and for the many other factors that can make people feel better. Weaker designs — open-label pilots with no control group, case reports, or surveys of existing users — can suggest a direction for further research, but they cannot prove a product works.

Next, check the dose and the population. A study that tested an ingredient at 500 mg a day in healthy young adults tells you nothing about a product containing 50 mg, or about older adults, or about people with a particular condition. Research is specific: the dose, the form of the ingredient, the duration of the trial and the characteristics of the participants all matter. A claim that ignores these details is borrowing authority it has not earned.

Then look at funding. Research paid for by the manufacturer is not automatically wrong, but it carries a higher risk of bias — in the design of the study, in the choice of outcomes to report, and in whether negative results ever see the light of day. Independent replication, where a different team with no financial interest reaches the same conclusion, is the strongest evidence available, and it is also the rarest. When a brand cites only its own funded research, treat the claim with appropriate caution.

Also check whether the study was published and peer reviewed. A conference poster, a company white paper or an unpublished internal report has not been through the scrutiny that peer review provides. That does not make it false, but it does mean it should be treated as preliminary rather than as proof. And check the date: a study from fifteen years ago may have been superseded, and a formula studied then may not be the formula sold now.

Finally, look at the whole picture rather than a single paper. A single positive study is a starting point, not a verdict. Systematic reviews, which combine the results of many studies, and meta-analyses, which pool their data, give a far better sense of what the evidence actually says — including the studies that found no effect, which individual brands rarely mention.

A citation is not evidence. It is a pointer to evidence — and the evidence itself is what you need to read.
